# Wayne Heim — Centering Narrative
### The Master Positioning Document
*For use in pitches, artist statements, queries, interviews, and institutional outreach*

---

## THE CORE STATEMENT
*(The one paragraph everything else builds from)*

Much of the mythology of the American West focuses on where the frontier ended. Wayne Heim's work is interested in where it began — in the people who carried their histories, losses, beliefs, and identities westward from places like Pennsylvania, Ohio, New York, and Europe itself. Working from Ohio and collaborating with historians and living-history communities across the Eastern United States, Heim traces the emotional and historical DNA of the frontier back to its origins — and finds that the West was not a place people were born into, but a story people chose to enter, at enormous cost.

## THE ORIGIN STORY
*(For human interest pieces, local press, personal interviews)*

Wayne Heim grew up in Star Valley, Wyoming — SVHS class of 1986 — in the shadow of a landscape that shaped him before he had words for it. Like many kids from the valley, he left. He studied fine art and Medical Illustration at the Cleveland Institute of Art in Cleveland, Ohio, graduating with a BFA and what would have been a minor in photography had the program allowed it.

For over thirty-five years he built an award-winning career as a medical illustrator — creating high-end 3D illustrations and animations that explain complex surgical procedures and medical devices to doctors and patients worldwide. He presented and was recognized at conferences across the globe. At its core, every piece of that work was the same discipline: take something invisible and complicated, and make it understood in a single image.

The West never left him. Every camera he ever picked up pointed at a story.

About fifteen years ago, his son's Boy Scout troop attended a local WWII reenactment. Wayne went along with a camera. By the end of that afternoon, something had locked into place. Not the spectacle of the event — but the question it raised: could you take a photograph that showed not just what a historical moment *looks like*, but what it might have *felt like* to be inside it?

That question has driven everything since.

He is married to his wife Chris. They have four children — one son and three daughters — and have called Ohio home for decades. The valley never really let go.

## THE ARTISTIC PRACTICE
*(For editorial features, gallery notes, collector materials)*

Heim calls his work **painterly photography** — but the phrase requires unpacking.

The photograph is the foundation, the prepared ground. The camera records the moment. From there Heim works the way a painter or illustrator does: shaping light as emotional truth, constructing atmosphere, moving color, texture, and composition in service of the narrative the image must carry. His thirty-five years of medical illustration trained him to guide a viewer's eye through a complex image — to make them focus on exactly what matters without knowing they are being guided. That discipline is fully operational in every Western image he makes.

Each image is what he calls **an interrupted moment of a bigger story**. The frame does not open and close. It opens — and stays open. The viewer steps inside the way they enter a film or stand before a great painting: forgetting the medium, forgetting that these are reenactors, simply present in the weight of the moment.

To deepen that engagement, Heim writes a short narrative for each image — a story fragment that provides hidden context without completing the picture. Title, written story, and image work together as a unified system designed to pull the viewer past the frame's edge and require them to write the rest of the story themselves, using their own experience and emotion.

He calls the result a **One-Image Movie** — a piece that plays differently for every person who stands before it, and keeps opening every time they return.

**The Engrained Series**

The most unusual element of the practice is the Engrained format. Engrained works are printed directly onto wood panels, selectively allowing the grain — the physical record of time, weather, and seasons survived — to read through the image as active texture and depth. The wood's story and the image's story become one object.

Tree rings are time. To print a historical narrative onto a surface that carries its own historical record is not a stylistic choice. It is an argument about the nature of history itself. No two Engrained panels are identical, because no two panels have the same rings. The edition is the original. History printed on history.

## THE OHIO PARADOX
*(The reframe — for use when the geography question arises)*

Everyone assumes the West belongs to the West. But the cowboys, the settlers, the frontier people — they came from Ohio, Pennsylvania, New York, Ireland, Germany, and a dozen other places that never appear in Western mythology. The emotional and historical DNA of the frontier is Eastern and European. The West was not an origin. It was a destination.

Heim works with reenactors and historical experts from across the Eastern United States — including some of the most rigorous living-history communities in the country. Bedford, Pennsylvania hosts a mid-1880s frontier town with over twenty period-correct log cabins and structures, populated by historians obsessing over authentic material culture and period detail. These are not hobbyists. They are living historical collaborators preserving the migratory memory of the frontier.

The geography is not abstract. Heim lives in Chesterland, Ohio — one town over from Kirtland, which served as a documented staging point for westward migration communities in the 1830s before those same groups continued toward Missouri, Illinois, and eventually the frontier West. The migration routes that fed the frontier ran through his backyard. The eastern origins of the West are not a theory here. They are the landscape.

The deeper realization that drives the work: the West didn't emerge fully formed west of the Mississippi. Its emotional and cultural roots run through Ohio, Pennsylvania, New York, immigrant communities, religious migrations, and people searching for a new identity long before they ever reached the plains. People leaving failed farms, industrial cities, economic collapse, old countries, old names — heading toward reinvention at enormous personal cost.

The frontier was not just a place. It was a state of transition.

Which is why the One-Image Movie structure fits the Western subject so naturally. Western stories are fundamentally threshold stories — identity under pressure, the moment before the crossing, the weight of what is being left behind and what may never arrive. That is the suspended moment every one of Heim's images is built around.

Working from Ohio is not a geographic liability. It is a perspective. Heim is working from the same place the West actually came from.

## THE SUBJECT MATTER
*(The why — for collectors, institutions, editorial)*

Walk through a major Western art show. The technical accomplishment is extraordinary. Cowboys rendered with photographic precision. Landscapes of breathtaking scale. And yet — something is absent.

The stories are closed. Every image delivers everything on first viewing and asks nothing in return.

Heim's work goes where contemporary Western art has largely stopped going: into the difficult, the human, the morally unresolved. Stories of frontier women in positions of power. Native figures carrying full human complexity rather than romantic reduction. The moral cost of the journey West. The doctor who will likely die honoring his oath. The woman holding herself together inside a covered wagon so the children don't see her break.

He calls them **the stories of the ones history moved too fast to name.** The armature beneath the iconic West. The sacrifices and losses that made the legends possible — and that the legends never explain.

His goal is not critique. It is foundation. You cannot fully understand what created the heroes of the West until you see what those heroes were built on top of. That is where the true Western spirit lives — in the endurance, the grief, the moral weight, the keeping on.

And maybe — through that discovery — the people standing in front of these images find something in themselves. If those pioneers could carry what they carried and keep going, so can we.

## THE ART HISTORICAL POSITION
*(For institutional pitches, curators, serious editorial — use selectively)*

The narrative tradition that gave Western American art its cultural weight — the unresolved moment, the story held in tension, the image that makes the viewer feel the weight of what isn't shown — was largely abandoned by the contemporary Western canon in favor of technical virtuosity and heroic spectacle.

Remington and Russell were making cinematic images before Hollywood existed. John Ford studied Remington's paintings as visual storyboards. The cinematic language of the Western film didn't originate in cinema — it originated in paint, and in the discipline of leaving the story deliberately unresolved.

That tradition has a continuation. It arrives through a different medium, with a broader human record, and with the full structural sophistication of the One-Image Movie architecture. But the lineage is unbroken.

Wayne Heim's work is a return to that discipline — through a new medium, with a broader human record, and with the full structural sophistication of the One-Image Movie architecture.
